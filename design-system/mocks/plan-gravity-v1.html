Plan gravity — the 9 → 1 made visible.

This app's entire job is turning nine scattered options into one decision. Today that happens in a form: you press buttons and a number goes up. Gravity makes the screen do what the group is doing — options start scattered, and pull into alignment as agreement emerges.

The load-bearing decision is that this is layout, not physics. There is no simulation loop, no per-frame force integration, no requestAnimationFrame running for the length of a round. Votes arrive as discrete events over Realtime, so each vote is one transition — a handful over a whole round, not sixty a second. That is what makes it survivable on a mid-range Android, which is the device a share-link guest actually opens.

Try it: click any card to cast a vote. Watch the scatter collapse as the group agrees.

Decided · you're going

What is actually animating. Two CSS custom properties and nothing else: --c (agreement, 0→1) collapses each card's --off scatter and tightens the grid gap; --lead scales the front-runner by 4.5%. Every card animates transform only — compositor work, no layout, no paint. The gap is the one property that costs a layout pass, and it changes at most once per vote. No blur, no shadow, no ambient motion, nothing running when nobody is voting. These three numbers are structural facts about the implementation, not a benchmark — a live frame-timer was tried here and removed, because requestAnimationFrame does not run reliably under browser automation and a metric that silently reads zero is worse than no metric. The real budget has to be measured on a real device, and the spec says so rather than quoting a number from a laptop.

The moment

What a vote feels like

Three beats, and only three — the owner has asked twice for restraint, so this is not "everything moves". Each beat carries information that was previously only a number.

1 · The vote lands. Your avatar leaves the waiting tray and travels onto the card you picked. It is one element moving between two places — in the real build that is Motion's layoutId, which is exactly the case it exists for. This replaces a counter incrementing: a number says how many, a face says who, and who is the reason the group is on this screen together.

2 · Weight accrues. The front-runner grows 4.5% and its hairline goes solid #969A9E — no colour is spent, the palette is frozen at §24, and the edge value is already legal (6.39 on canvas, 5.41 on card). The word "leading" stays in the markup, because §23.4b's rule holds: size is never the only signal.

3 · The round closes. Losers fold rather than vanish — 340ms, opacity and a 6% scale-down, so the eye can follow where they went. The winner takes the full width and its name steps to 2rem, and then hands off to WinnerReveal, which is already cinematic. Gravity lands into that moment rather than competing with it.

Why this one, and not the other eleven

Gravity is the only interaction on that list that could not exist in another app. Everything else — parallax, magnetic buttons, cursor followers, page transitions — is portable premium polish that would look identical bolted onto a banking app. This one is the product's own logic, rendered. Nine things becoming one thing is not a metaphor here; it is the feature.

  1. It costs no colour. The palette is frozen at §24 and nothing here touches it. Energy comes from position, scale and timing — §23.4's finding, applied.
  2. It works with no photographs. Gravity is positional. 76 of 82 spots have no image and this is unaffected by that, which is the test D1's premise failed.
  3. It is event-driven, not frame-driven. The thing that kills interactions like this on mobile is a physics loop; there isn't one. Nothing animates unless somebody votes.
  4. Reduced motion keeps the meaning. Cards sit where agreement says they sit, with no travel. Gravity becomes a state rather than a journey — nothing informational is lost.

What I did not build: ambient drift, cursor attraction, tilt on the option cards, or anything that moves while the user is reading. A round can sit open for hours; a screen that never stops moving is a screen nobody can read a menu on.
